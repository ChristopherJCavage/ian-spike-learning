## Chris Cavage

Ian is six, and most of what we build starts at our kitchen table with the
SPIKE™ sets we already have. He builds every model before anyone else sees
it. If he can't do a step, the step changes.

Every Saturday morning we take him to the
[Contemporary Chinese School of Arizona](https://www.ccsarizona.org/) in
Phoenix. My wife is Chinese, and Ian is there for Chinese class. After
class, the school runs a LEGO® robotics club for four- to eight-year-olds.

I got involved the way most parents do: I asked the coach if there was
anything I could help with. The SPIKE™ App comes with good lessons, but a
semester is long, and a room of six-year-olds needs more variety than that.
So I keep in touch with the coach, and when the club needs more builds, we
talk about what would make sense.

By day I'm a Sr. Director of Engineering, leading the global Platform &
Firmware division at Axon Enterprise, Inc., at its headquarters in
Scottsdale, Arizona. We support almost every physical camera and IoT
product you can see on Axon's website. Before that I spent a decade at
Digital Control in Kent, Washington, building the locators that steer
drills under roads and rivers, and before that I was at ENSCO outside D.C.,
working in the defense industry. That's twenty years of software for
hardware that has to keep working after it's been dropped, rained on, or
buried. Most of my week
now is people and budgets. A kitchen table covered in bricks, with a
six-year-old asking why, is the part that still feels like engineering.

The builds are public because the kits are on a clock. LEGO® Education
stopped selling SPIKE™ in 2026, and plenty of clubs like ours already have
the boxes and not much budget for anything new. The details are on
[Kits](/kits). The short version is that these kits were always about
teaching a kid that a machine does what you told it, not what you meant,
and they're still good at it.

Everything here is free. If a step is wrong or a part count doesn't match
your box, tell me.
